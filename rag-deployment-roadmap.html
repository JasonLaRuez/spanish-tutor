One more thread, layered on top rather than counted in the four above: SQL. It isn't a gap in the same sense — the resume already claims "working knowledge" — but it's the single most requested skill in the two data-facing title families (75% of Data Analyst postings, common in Data Engineer), and "working knowledge" undersells what actually needs to be shown. The word bank's schema, the recommender's core ranking query, and dbt's models are all SQL by construction — this project is where that claim gets real.

Objectives

  1. 1Ship a tutor with three skills — conversation (topic-focused, pre-teaches vocabulary before it's needed), lyrics/figurative translation, and reading (short stories and sequence-aware multi-chapter books) — that you'd actually keep using after the project is "done."
  2. 2Build a real word bank: a persistent, structured store of vocabulary you've learned, read before every response and written to whenever something new gets taught.
  3. 3Build a comprehensible-input recommender: given the word bank and a set of candidate topics/songs/stories/chapters, default to whichever needs the fewest new words — unless you explicitly ask for something specific. This is a direct implementation of Krashen's i+1 hypothesis, not folk intuition about how learning works.
  4. 4Design and validate a custom evaluation framework — vocabulary adherence, teaching completeness, translation naturalness, and recommendation quality. The direct extension of the dissertation's evaluation discipline into new territory.
  5. 5Deploy it behind a containerized, real endpoint with basic observability.
  6. 6Run two scheduled jobs against real state: spaced-repetition recalculation and content-difficulty re-indexing.
  7. 7Produce a documented, public artifact — with any copyrighted lyrics or books kept private — that a resume bullet or cover-letter evidence block can honestly point to.
  8. 8Write real, demonstrable SQL throughout — schema design, the recommender's core ranking query, and dbt models — upgrading the resume's "working knowledge of SQL" into something you can actually defend in an interview.

The dashed boxes are the same two tables — word bank and difficulty index — read and written throughout, not separate stores per stage. A book's chapters are just difficulty-indexed content items like any song or story, with one added constraint: the recommender can rank which book to start or continue, but never suggests chapter 7 before chapter 6 of the same book.

Two things wrap around all three loops: an orchestrator (dbt or Airflow) runs the spaced-repetition recalculation against the word bank, and separately re-indexes content difficulty whenever new corpus items are added. A deployment layer (Docker + FastAPI, hosted on the cloud platform) puts all three skills behind a real endpoint instead of a notebook cell, with a React web UI on top of it (built from Phase 2.5 onward).

Data source options

The one real constraint here is copyright, not availability — worth deciding up front rather than discovering it at the last phase.

Recommended primary corpus

Tatoeba. CC-licensed, community-built corpus of translated sentence pairs, explicitly built for language learners. Grounds the conversation skill and grammar/usage examples at a given difficulty level.

Real, well-known, free, and legally clean to redistribute in a public repo — unlike almost anything else on this list.
Topic vocabulary

Subtlex-ESP, a freely available Spanish word-frequency corpus (built from subtitles), for grounding "what vocabulary is actually relevant to gardening/sports/weather" rather than trusting the model's unverified guess.

Same instinct as the LittleSIS data-integrity story — check the claim against a real source before you teach it.
Reading skill

Project Gutenberg's Spanish-language collection for short stories and public-domain books — real, well-known, explicitly public domain, no licensing ambiguity.

Solves the reading skill's copyright question outright rather than needing a private/public split, for anything sourced from it.
Keep private

Song lyrics, and any book under copyright. Fine for your own local use; not fine baked into a public repo or corpus, chapter-split or not.

Keep these as your own local, gitignored input at runtime. The public repo ships with Gutenberg texts and public-domain poetry as the demo equivalent.

What "done" looks like

  • A public repo someone could clone and actually run, with a README that explains the architecture in two paragraphs — and no copyrighted lyrics or books anywhere in it
  • A written evaluation of the custom metrics (vocabulary adherence, teaching completeness, translation naturalness, recommendation quality), with real numbers, not just "it works"
  • A live or recorded demo of a topic conversation: pre-teaching happening first, then the model staying inside your word bank, then correctly teaching a new word when it has to
  • A demo of the recommender picking the lowest-new-word option by default, and correctly honoring an explicit request instead
  • A handful of real SQL queries — the schema, the recommender's ranking query, the dbt models, the analytics queries — you can walk through line by line in an interview
  • One new, true sentence for a cover letter's evidence blocks — the same 300-350 word module format already in use
  • Something you're actually still using in week 16
Honesty checkpoints

Nothing here gets added to any resume variant until it's actually true — the same discipline already applied to every bullet on file.

And separately: don't let a copyrighted lyric sheet or a copyrighted book end up committed to a public repo, chapter-split or not — chunking a book changes its architecture, not its license. Keep anything copyrighted local and gitignored from the start.
